## Findings

**MN-1. Denominator: ticket and comment say "over the ten", the code averages over the stated.** Domain: micronutrients/engine. Classification: documentation or requirement gap (confirmed mismatch between MM-380 line 34, `FoodGradeRule.ts:16-18` and `nutrientDensityScore.ts:63`). Severity: high (it decides MN-2). Confidence: high (read both). Expected: one stated method. Observed: two. Remediation: state in MM-380, the rule's comment, `docs/food-grades.md` and the register that the mean is over the stated nutrients (the only reading that honours MM-310, "a missing value is never zero"); fix the broccoli test comment at `foodGrade.test.ts:95` to say five stated → 31.3. Acceptance (proposed): "Given a food stating 5 of the 10, Then the mean's denominator is 5."

**MN-2. A mean over the stated set rewards a label that declares a voluntary nutrient it is rich in; the minimum of five selects for exactly those labels.** Domain: micronutrients. Classification: evidence-supported domain concern. Severity: high. Confidence: high on the mechanism (label rule and code), medium on magnitude (approximate values). Evidence: 21 CFR 101.9(c)(8)(ii) makes vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium the only mandatory vitamins and minerals; the others are declared when added, claimed, or at the maker's choice (LII, below). So a standard post-2020 US label states four of the ten; the fifth that lifts it over `micronutrientsMinStated` is, by construction, one the maker chose to declare, usually because the food is rich in it. Calculation on the chair's own orange-juice inputs (`gradeCalibrationFoods.ts:68`): all ten stated → mean 14.0% → 4 points; label's four (K 9.5, Ca 1.9, Fe 2.5, D 0) → not counted under min 5; label's four plus vitamin C declared → (9.5+1.9+2.5+0+100)/5 = 22.8% → **6 points, two more than the complete profile earns**. Broccoli in the test itself shows it: five stated → 31.3%, ten stated → 22.4%. User impact: the same food graded higher from a sparser source. Remediation (proposed, concrete): keep the mean and the 100% cap, and **bound the component's points by breadth**: points = min(points from the mean, number of stated nutrients at ≥ 5% of the DV per 100 kcal). 5% per 100 kcal has a plain meaning the popover can carry: at that density, 2,000 kcal of the food supplies the whole Daily Value. Effect on the calibration set (all ten stated; my arithmetic on the chair's inputs): broccoli 6 (breadth 8), spinach 6 (8), lentils 3 (5), Greek yogurt 3 (4), tofu 3 (5), tuna 4 (5), chicken 1 (2), salmon 4 → **2** (breadth 2: D, B12), egg 2 (5), beef 3 (3), avocado 1 (3), banana 1 (4), oats 1 (3), pasta 0, deli ham 2 (3), white bread 1 (3), whole milk 3 (5), rice 0, plain yogurt 2 (4), almonds 1 (1), pizza 1 (4), orange juice 4 (4), peanut butter 0, cheddar 1 (4), chips 0, ice cream 1 (2), cola/oil/chocolate/butter 0. Only salmon moves, 9 → 7, and 7 is still B+ (floor 7): **no calibration letter changes**. The branded orange juice above falls to 2 (K, C), an under-count instead of an inflation. Alternative (simpler, bigger calibration shift): drop the mean and score the breadth count directly (max 6): tuna 4 → 5, salmon 4 → 2, chicken 1 → 2, egg 2 → 5, lentils 3 → 5; I prefer the bound because it keeps the draft's letters. Verification: unrun; a test on the orange-juice three-way case above.

**MN-3. Minimum stated of five excludes every standard US label; four matches the law.** Domain: micronutrients. Classification: evidence-supported domain concern. Severity: medium (becomes high once a format 2 barcode pack ships, since 456k barcode foods are the bulk of the pack, `docs/food-grades.md` lines 62-63). Confidence: high. Post-2020 labels state D, Ca, Fe, K (four); pre-2020 records state Ca, Fe, C and vitamin A in IU, which the pipeline drops (three). Under five, a branded food's twin of a generic food loses the component outright: on the calibration inputs, the generic earns 0-6 points that the branded never can (greens 6, tuna/salmon/OJ 4, lentils/yogurt/tofu/beef/milk 3), and its popover says vitamins and minerals "aren't stated", which is untrue of the four it states. Remediation (proposed): `micronutrientsMinStated: 4`, with MN-2's bound so a sparse set cannot inflate. Label-only outcomes under four + bound: broccoli 3 (K, Ca, Fe at density), spinach 3, lentils 2, Greek yogurt 2, tofu 2, tuna 2, salmon 1, milk 2, chicken/apple/beef 0: an under-count of 0-3 against the complete profile, never an over-count. Owner decision D1.

**MN-4. Fortification counts as the label states it; the component cannot tell added from intrinsic.** Classification: evidence-supported domain concern (property, not defect). Severity: medium. Confidence: high. The DV framework and the label treat a fortificant as the nutrient (21 CFR 101.9(c)(8)(ii): added nutrients must be declared). Illustrative arithmetic (typical fortified cereal, 375 kcal/100 g, about 25% DV per 39 g serving of Fe, Zn, A, C, D, B12, folate): about 17% each per 100 kcal, mean about 13% → 4 points; with 8 g fiber the sugars waiver (`FoodGradeRule.ts:56-57`) also forgives 37 g sugars, and the food lands B-; without the fiber, C. Fortified soy milk earns about 5 points where an unfortified one earns 1. MN-2's bound does not change this (breadth is real). No fix exists inside the owner's constraints; the honest course is to record it in the register and `docs/food-grades.md`, and to refer the waiver interaction to the nutrition role. Owner decision D3.

**MN-5. The Daily Values are the right universal reference; record their two known gaps.** Classification: documentation gap (no defect). Severity: low. Confidence: high. Why DV over `referenceAmount`: the owner fixed one grade for everyone; `referenceAmount` varies by sex and age and is undefined under 19, so it would make the letter personal and absent for teenagers; the DV is also what a label's %DV is computed against, so branded values back-converted from %DV round-trip exactly. Gaps to record in the register row for `FoodGradeRule.dailyValues`: potassium 4,700 mg is the 2005 AI, not the 2019 NASEM AI of 3,400/2,600 that `referenceAmount.ts:22` uses, so potassium's share is understated by about 28-45% for adults; calcium 1,300 mg is the 9-18-year RDA, understating adults' share by 8-23%. Vitamin D 20 µg and iron 18 mg are the highest adult RDAs. None changes an order within the calibration set. Also make the comment at `FoodGradeRule.ts:29-30` say "µg RAE" for vitamin A and "µg DFE" for folate, the pack's units (`Micronutrient.ts:41-43`).

**MN-6. Sodium's exclusion from the component is right.** Classification: no finding (confirmation). Sodium's 2,300 mg is a DRV limit under 101.9(c)(9), not an RDI to reach; counting it in a "share reached" mean would reward salt. Cholesterol, sugars and saturated fat are likewise limits and are rightly outside `GradeMicronutrient`.

**MN-7. The popover's not-counted sentence is false for a food that states one to four.** Domain: micronutrients/voice. Classification: usability problem found by inspection (`FoodGrade.tsx:31`). Severity: low. Confidence: high. "Vitamins and minerals aren't stated for this food" will be read as "this food has none" on a label that states four. Proposed words (MM-108 voice, no moral words): when fewer than the minimum: "Only 2 of the 10 vitamins and minerals the grade looks at are stated for this food, so they weren't counted." When counted from a subset (if D1 adopts four): "Vitamins and minerals counted from the 4 stated." This needs the stated count on `FoodGradeValue` (today only `notCounted: GradeOmission[]`), a small type change the edit owner should weigh.

**MN-8. A stated 0 is "under 2% of the DV per serving", not nothing; fine for density, say so.** Classification: documentation gap. Severity: low. Under 101.9(c)(8)(iii) an amount under 2% of the RDI "may be declared by a zero"; a label 0 is therefore a small amount, at most about 2% per serving, which per 100 kcal can be a few percent for a low-calorie serving. Acceptable for this component; record in the register that a label zero is counted as stated and small.

**MN-9. Tofu sits 0.03 above a threshold.** Classification: unverified hypothesis requiring investigation (measurement). Tofu's mean is 7.53% against the 7.5 threshold; a 1% change in any value moves it a point. Referred to measurement for the sensitivity view; not a micronutrient defect.

**MN-10 (MM-381). What each source can be expected to carry of the thirteen, and why.** Classification: evidence-supported domain concern, with expectations not measurements (no build has run).
- **USDA SR Legacy** (final release 04/2018; values "from analyses, imputations, and published literature", FDC About page): a near-complete profile for almost all of its foods, so all thirteen for the great majority, with vitamin D and B12 the ones most often absent on older entries. The fullest present source for the ten; expect most SR Legacy foods to pass any minimum.
- **USDA Foundation** (updated April and October): its own documentation says "Foundation Foods does not provide data on all nutrients"; newer foods are analysed for "important nutrients in that food", and most profiles are proximates plus at least one other nutrient. Expect partial sets, vitamin D rare, so a Foundation food will often fail a minimum of five, or pass with a sparse set. Because generic foods are not deduplicated (`PackBuilder.ts:122-136`), a Foundation "Broccoli, raw" and an SR Legacy one will both appear, with different grades and one saying "not counted". Referred to integrations.
- **USDA Branded** (label values from manufacturers, updated monthly, downloads April and October; "a missing value does not mean zero"; pre-2020 %DV-only records converted with the 1994 DV table, per its documentation): post-2020 records: sodium, fiber, total sugars, saturated fat, cholesterol, vitamin D, calcium, iron, potassium (nine of thirteen) for nearly all; vitamin A, C, magnesium, zinc, B12, folate only when added, claimed or volunteered. Pre-2020 records: vitamin C, calcium, iron, sodium, sugars, saturated fat, cholesterol; vitamin A in IU (dropped); D and K only when volunteered.
- **Open Food Facts (US)**: label fields, grams per 100 g; its field list is the same shape for every nutrient but the wiki states nutrient lists differ by country and time; vitamin A and folate are refused by MM-307; expect sugars, saturated fat and sodium commonly, D/B12/Mg/Zn rarely. No published completeness figure was found; must be measured at the build.
- **USDA FNDDS (Survey)**: 65 nutrients and food components (ARS), "derived from the Foundation Food and SR Legacy databases" (FDC About), for every food reported in What We Eat in America; FNDDS 2021-2023 in the FDC CSV download dated 10/2024 (200 MB zipped, 1.6 GB CSV), `data_type` survey food in the same `food.csv`/`food_nutrient.csv`/`food_portion.csv` shape with the same nutrient ids, so it joins `extract.sql`'s generic branch as a third `UNION ALL` with no new SQL shape. Because FNDDS values are recipe-calculated, every food is expected to carry all thirteen, which closes the "stated" gap for generic foods and adds mixed dishes and restaurant foods as eaten. **I could not open USDA's own statement of completeness** (the FNDDS PDF would not render here; the NCC comparison page returned 500); treat "no missing values" as expected, verified by `coverage.txt` at the first build. Caveats for the register: FNDDS "stated" means USDA's calculated estimate, not an analysis of that food; survey-style names ("NS as to cooking method") will stress `preparationLinks`; simple foods duplicate SR Legacy unless the generic branch dedups. Licence text not read (the ARS page says free download; public-domain status is for integrations to confirm). Recommendation: yes, FNDDS fills the gap for generic foods; rebuild in format 2 with it; publish only after `coverage.txt` shows, per source, how many foods state each of the ten and how many reach the minimum.
